# Recovering farm ground after war damage

These are planning steps, not permission to enter a damaged area. **Stop work and keep people and livestock away** if there may be unexploded ordnance, chemical containers, buried cables, or unstable structures. Ask the local mine-action authority, fire service, environmental regulator, and an agronomist to inspect first. Costs below are rough **USD per hectare** planning ranges, not bids. They use the cited public rate sources and the conversion **1 hectare = 2.471 acres**; prices vary by country, access, depth, waste class, and season.

## Crater

1. **Mark the crater and get a safety clearance.** Fence it, photograph it from a safe distance, and have a qualified mine-action or explosives team clear the area before anyone approaches. **Cost: $25–$250/ha for an initial survey and marking allowance.** This is a planning allowance derived from the UNMAS land-release workflow; it is not a universal clearance price. Source: [UNMAS, International Mine Action Standards—Land Release](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/).
2. **Test the soil around the crater.** Take a representative sample from the crater spoil and from the surrounding field, and test pH, salts, nutrients, petroleum/metals, and any suspected explosive residues. Do not spread spoil until the results are reviewed. **Cost: $40–$150/ha**, assuming one to three composite samples at **$15–$50 each** plus collection; the per-sample allowance is based on the [University of Minnesota soil-testing guidance and fee schedule](https://soiltest.cfans.umn.edu/).
3. **Remove only unsafe or unsuitable spoil, then backfill in lifts.** Keep contaminated soil separate; use clean, similar-textured fill, place it in thin layers, and compact each layer so the field does not settle later. **Cost: $1,000–$4,000/ha of affected area** for light excavation, hauling, fill, and grading. This is calculated from the equipment and labor operation rates in [Iowa State University, Estimated Costs of Crop Production](https://www.extension.iastate.edu/agdm/crops/pdf/a3-10.pdf), multiplied by 2.471 acres/ha; obtain a local excavation quote.
4. **Rebuild the topsoil layer.** Spread saved topsoil or verified clean topsoil over the repaired area, then lightly incorporate compost only if the soil test and agronomist support it. **Cost: $750–$3,000/ha of affected area** for spreading and incorporation, using the same Iowa State field-operation rate basis: [Iowa State University, Estimated Costs of Crop Production](https://www.extension.iastate.edu/agdm/crops/pdf/a3-10.pdf).
5. **Control erosion and replant.** Shape the surface to drain safely, seed a temporary cover, and inspect after heavy rain. **Cost: $250–$900/ha**, based on seeding and field-preparation operation rates in [Iowa State University, Estimated Costs of Crop Production](https://www.extension.iastate.edu/agdm/crops/pdf/a3-10.pdf) and the establishment approach in [USDA NRCS, Conservation Practice Standards](https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/resources/guides-and-instructions/conservation-practice-standards).

## Burn

1. **Wait for the fire service to declare the site safe.** Keep out until hot spots, weakened buildings, gas cylinders, ammunition, and ash hazards have been checked. **Cost: $0–$250/ha for a farm-level inspection allowance.** The amount is a planning allowance; the safety sequence follows [USDA Forest Service, fire management guidance](https://www.fs.usda.gov/managing-land/fire).
2. **Map the burn and separate ash, debris, and soil.** Photograph the area, mark drainage paths, and keep ash, treated timber, plastics, batteries, and metal out of feed and cropland. **Cost: $100–$600/ha** for hand sorting, loading, and farm cleanup, using the Iowa State machinery/labor rate basis: [Iowa State University, Estimated Costs of Crop Production](https://www.extension.iastate.edu/agdm/crops/pdf/a3-10.pdf).
3. **Test ash and soil before spreading or grazing.** Test pH, electrical conductivity, nutrients, and likely contaminants from structures, treated wood, fuel, or firefighting foam. **Cost: $40–$150/ha**, based on one to three composite samples at **$15–$50 each** from the [University of Minnesota soil-testing guidance and fee schedule](https://soiltest.cfans.umn.edu/).
4. **Stabilize bare ground before the next storm.** Use straw or mulch where appropriate, contour or wattles on slopes, and a temporary cover crop or approved native mix. **Cost: $250–$1,000/ha**, based on field seeding and cover-establishment operation rates in [Iowa State University, Estimated Costs of Crop Production](https://www.extension.iastate.edu/agdm/crops/pdf/a3-10.pdf) and [USDA NRCS, Conservation Practice Standards](https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/resources/guides-and-instructions/conservation-practice-standards).
5. **Restore soil structure and return animals gradually.** Avoid heavy traffic on wet ash-affected soil, add only amendments recommended by the soil test, and wait for clean feed and water results before grazing. **Cost: $100–$750/ha** for shallow tillage, amendment spreading, and reseeding, using the Iowa State field-operation rates: [Iowa State University, Estimated Costs of Crop Production](https://www.extension.iastate.edu/agdm/crops/pdf/a3-10.pdf).

## Trench

1. **Survey the trench and check for explosives or buried services.** Fence steep edges, keep machinery away from unstable banks, and get a competent person to check for collapse, cables, pipes, and ordnance. **Cost: $25–$250/ha for a survey and marking allowance**, following [UNMAS, International Mine Action Standards—Land Release](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/).
2. **Plan water control before filling.** Divert clean upslope water, repair blocked outlets, and prevent the trench from becoming a new erosion channel. **Cost: $250–$1,200/ha**, based on grading, shallow ditching, and field-operation rates in [Iowa State University, Estimated Costs of Crop Production](https://www.extension.iastate.edu/agdm/crops/pdf/a3-10.pdf) and the drainage/stabilization principles in [USDA NRCS, Conservation Practice Standards](https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/resources/guides-and-instructions/conservation-practice-standards).
3. **Remove trash and unsuitable material.** Separate metal, treated timber, fuel containers, and visibly contaminated soil from clean earth; use a licensed waste route for anything hazardous. **Cost: $500–$3,000/ha of affected area**, calculated from excavation, loading, and hauling operation rates in [Iowa State University, Estimated Costs of Crop Production](https://www.extension.iastate.edu/agdm/crops/pdf/a3-10.pdf); disposal fees require a local quote.
4. **Fill in thin, compacted layers.** Use clean subsoil below and saved topsoil above; compact each lift and avoid burying organic debris that will settle. **Cost: $1,000–$4,000/ha of affected area**, using the Iowa State excavation, hauling, grading, and field-operation rate basis: [Iowa State University, Estimated Costs of Crop Production](https://www.extension.iastate.edu/agdm/crops/pdf/a3-10.pdf).
5. **Rebuild the topsoil and seed a cover.** Spread topsoil, leave the surface level with the surrounding field, seed a cover, and check settlement after rain. **Cost: $750–$3,000/ha**, based on topsoil spreading, tillage, and seeding rates in [Iowa State University, Estimated Costs of Crop Production](https://www.extension.iastate.edu/agdm/crops/pdf/a3-10.pdf).

## Contamination

1. **Isolate the area and stop food, feed, and water use.** Fence it, label it, prevent dust and runoff, and do not taste, burn, bury, or move suspect material. **Cost: $50–$400/ha** for temporary fencing, signs, and runoff controls, using the field-labor/material rate basis in [Iowa State University, Estimated Costs of Crop Production](https://www.extension.iastate.edu/agdm/crops/pdf/a3-10.pdf). A regulator may require more.
2. **Identify the contaminant and map its boundary.** Hire an environmental professional to design a sampling plan; collect separate samples from source material, soil, water, and produce or feed where relevant. **Cost: $150–$750/ha** for a screening investigation, based on one to three composite soil samples at **$15–$50 each** plus field labor from the [University of Minnesota soil-testing guidance and fee schedule](https://soiltest.cfans.umn.edu/). Specialist chemical analyses can cost much more.
3. **Choose treatment with the regulator.** Options may include excavation and disposal, clean-cover soil, soil washing, bioremediation, monitored natural recovery, or no farming until monitoring confirms safety. **Cost: $2,500–$15,000/ha** for a shallow, localized excavation-and-clean-fill allowance, calculated from excavation/hauling rates in [Iowa State University, Estimated Costs of Crop Production](https://www.extension.iastate.edu/agdm/crops/pdf/a3-10.pdf); hazardous-waste disposal and treatment are excluded and must be quoted.
4. **Excavate, cap, or treat only under an approved plan.** Keep clean and dirty equipment separate, document where every load goes, and use licensed transport and disposal when required. **Cost: $2,500–$15,000/ha** for the same shallow excavation-and-clean-fill planning case; the remedy-selection and documentation process follows [US EPA, Superfund cleanup process](https://www.epa.gov/superfund/superfund-cleanup-process).
5. **Verify cleanup before returning the field to production.** Resample, compare results with the regulator’s agricultural standards, test irrigation water and crops if needed, and keep records of restrictions and follow-up dates. **Cost: $100–$600/ha per monitoring round**, based on two to twelve composite samples at **$15–$50 each** plus field collection from the [University of Minnesota soil-testing guidance and fee schedule](https://soiltest.cfans.umn.edu/).

### How to use these figures

The ranges are deliberately broad because a hectare of affected ground can mean a few square metres or the whole field. They are screening figures for a work plan. Before committing money, ask for written quotes that state: affected area and depth, soil volume, haul distance, fill source, waste classification, laboratory analytes, traffic restrictions, taxes, and who signs off the land for farming.
